# Design Psychology & Cognitive Science Reference Guide

This guide details the core psychological laws and cognitive science principles used for evaluating and designing user interfaces.

## 1. Gestalt Principles (Wertheimer, Koffka, Köhler, 1920s)
The human brain is wired to see structure, logic, and patterns. It helps us make sense of the world.
* **Proximity:** Elements near each other are perceived as grouped.
* **Similarity:** Elements sharing visual characteristics (color, shape, size) are perceived as related.
* **Continuity:** The eye follows smooth, continuous paths rather than abrupt changes.
* **Closure:** The brain completes incomplete shapes or patterns.
* **Figure-Ground:** The brain distinguishes between the object of focus (figure) and the background (ground).
* **Common Fate:** Elements moving in the same direction or at the same speed are grouped together.
* **Prägnanz (Law of Simplicity):** The brain processes complex shapes in the simplest way possible.

## 2. Hick's Law (William Edmund Hick, 1952)
**Formula:** $RT = a + b \log_2(n)$
The time it takes to make a decision increases logarithmically with the number and complexity of choices.
* **Application:** Use progressive disclosure. Break complex processes into steps. Categorize long lists.

## 3. Fitts's Law (Paul Fitts, 1954)
**Formula:** $MT = a + b \log_2(2D/W)$
The time required to rapidly move to a target area is a function of the ratio between the distance to the target ($D$) and the width of the target ($W$).
* **Application:** Make primary CTAs large. Place important actions on edges or corners of the screen (infinite target width). Ensure mobile touch targets are at least $44 \times 44$px.

## 4. Miller's Law (George A. Miller, 1956)
The average person can only keep $7 \pm 2$ items in their working memory.
* **Application:** Chunk information into smaller groups. Limit navigation menus to 5-7 items. Format phone numbers or credit cards in chunks (e.g., XXXX-XXXX).

## 5. Cognitive Load Theory (John Sweller, 1988)
Working memory has a limited capacity. Instructional design can be used to reduce cognitive load in learners.
* **Intrinsic Load:** The inherent difficulty of the task. (Manage it by breaking tasks down).
* **Extraneous Load:** Load generated by the way information is presented. (Minimize it by removing clutter).
* **Germane Load:** The cognitive effort put into creating a permanent store of knowledge (schema). (Maximize it by using consistent patterns).

## 6. Von Restorff Effect (Hedwig von Restorff, 1933)
Also known as the Isolation Effect. When multiple similar objects are present, the one that differs from the rest is most likely to be remembered.
* **Application:** Use accent colors for primary CTAs. Create visual hierarchy.

## 7. Serial Position Effect (Hermann Ebbinghaus)
Users have a propensity to best remember the first and last items in a series.
* **Primacy Effect:** Remembering the first items.
* **Recency Effect:** Remembering the last items.
* **Application:** Place the most important actions at the far left or far right of a navigation bar.

## 8. Jakob's Law (Jakob Nielsen)
Users spend most of their time on *other* sites. They prefer your site to work the same way as all the other sites they already know.
* **Application:** Follow standard design conventions (e.g., logo top left, search top right). Do not reinvent the wheel for basic interactions.

## 9. Doherty Threshold (Walter J. Doherty, 1982)
Productivity soars when a computer and its users interact at a pace ($<400$ms) that ensures neither has to wait on the other.
* **Application:** Use skeleton loaders, optimistic UI updates, and loading animations if backend processes take longer than 400ms.

## 10. Peak-End Rule (Daniel Kahneman, 1993)
People judge an experience largely based on how they felt at its peak (i.e., its most intense point) and at its end, rather than based on the total sum or average of every moment of the experience.
* **Application:** Focus on making the final step of a flow (e.g., checkout success, task completion) delightful and reassuring.
